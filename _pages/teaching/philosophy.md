I believe that students learn more effectively when they feel capable of tackling difficult ideas. My goal as a computer science educator is to help students build understanding, confidence, and independence. I want students to ask questions, try different approaches, and learn from mistakes without lowering expectations for the work they do.

My years teaching information technology in Ghana helped shape this belief. Some students approached spreadsheet software expecting it to be difficult. I regularly encouraged them to see it as manageable, then used relatable examples and guided practice to work through it. I noticed how positive framing could change their willingness to participate and persevere. I learned that encouragement is not a substitute for rigor. It can help students approach rigorous work with greater confidence.

I connect new concepts to what students already know. Analogies, demonstrations, discussion, and hands-on tasks can make abstract computing topics easier to understand. As a Graduate Teaching Assistant at The University of Alabama, I lead laboratory sessions for CS 200: Software Design and Engineering, where I help students practice skills such as version control, UML modeling, and collaborative development. I aim to explain not only how a technique works, but why and when it is useful.

I look for evidence of learning in students' explanations, questions, problem-solving, and ability to apply concepts independently. I use questions and feedback to identify misunderstandings and adjust how I teach. Participation matters, but I do not assume that visible enthusiasm alone proves learning.

Teaching also requires me to keep learning. I reflect on my methods, welcome candid feedback, and look for ways to improve both my technical knowledge and teaching practice. Ultimately, I want students to leave with sound computing skills and greater confidence in their ability to tackle unfamiliar problems.
